Through much of the 1990s, Katalin Karikó asked for money to study messenger RNA, and she kept hearing no. Her grant applications were rejected, and the University of Pennsylvania eventually demoted her ([Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/vaccine-pioneers-overcame-rejection-and-demotion-win-nobel)). Decades later, the work she struggled to fund became the foundation of the mRNA COVID-19 vaccines and earned her a share of the 2023 Nobel Prize.

It's tempting to read her story as one about shortsighted reviewers but hindsight is 20/20. The people who turned her down didn't know how the story would end. They were experts tasked with evaluating good science, and if you had asked them, they likely would have said they wanted bold new ideas. Most of us would say the same. We say we value innovation, we tell the people we work with that we want them to think differently, and we praise colleagues who challenge how things have always been done.

We tend to assume that if we value creativity, we'll recognize and welcome it when we encounter it. But wanting originality in principle and welcoming it in practice turn out to be two different things. In fact, some of the very things that make an idea original can also make us more likely to reject it.

# What gets in the way

A lot can get in the way of welcoming a new idea, and some researchers believe part of it is a bias we don't know we have. [Mueller, Melwani and Goncalo (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611421018) wanted to understand why organizations so often reject creative ideas while describing creativity as a goal. They suspected it works much like other implicit biases, where what we say we believe differs from the associations we carry underneath. When asked directly, people expressed positive attitudes toward creativity. On an implicit measure, though, people who had been made to feel uncertain showed more negative associations with creativity relative to practicality, and that bias made it harder for them to recognize a creative idea when they saw one.

Uncertainty sits at the center of this because the more an idea departs from what we've done before, the less we have to draw on when evaluating it. Other research points to what can make that uncertainty feel even bigger. [Lee, Chang and Choi (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1080/10400419.2017.1360061) found that people who were made to feel fear showed a stronger implicit bias against creativity and rated a creative idea as less creative, even though their stated attitudes didn't change. [Keith and colleagues (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1002/jocb.621) found that ideas felt riskiest when people couldn't see how they would be useful, something that may be particularly difficult to see in highly original ideas.

Knowing a field well doesn't make us immune. When [Boudreau and colleagues (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2285) had 142 expert faculty evaluate 150 research proposals, more novel proposals tended to receive lower scores, and reviewers whose own work sat closer to a proposal's topic scored it lower than reviewers from farther away. Expertise helps us recognize genuine limitations, but it can also make us more aware of the ways an unfamiliar idea departs from established practices. Some of the same knowledge that makes someone a valuable evaluator may also make it harder for them to see the potential in a different approach.

# Why the caution makes sense

None of this means the people saying no are wrong to be careful. Turning new ideas into successful innovations is difficult. Research estimates that around 40 percent of new products fail ([Castellion & Markham, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5885.2012.01009.x)), and in drug development, only about 14 percent of drugs that enter clinical trials go on to win FDA approval ([Wong, Siah & Lo, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/biostatistics/kxx069)). Organizations have legitimate reasons to be selective about where they invest their time, money, and resources.

The more novel an idea is, the less predictable its outcome can be. In a large study of scientific papers, [Wang, Veugelers, and Stephan (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2017.06.006) found that highly novel research was more likely than other work to become a major hit, but it also took longer to be recognized and carried greater uncertainty in its eventual impact. That uncertainty is part of what made the Netflix decision so hard to see clearly at the time. When its founders offered to sell the company to Blockbuster for $50 million in 2000, the startup was losing money and Blockbuster was about a hundred times its size ([Hastings, via Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2020/09/08/ceo-reed-hastings-on-how-netflix-beat-blockbuster)). Knowing what we know now, it's easy to criticize that decision, but at the time, there were legitimate reasons to be skeptical.

Our caution about the unfamiliar resembles other mental shortcuts we use to decide under uncertainty ([Tversky & Kahneman, 1974](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124)). Shortcuts exist because they help. They let us move through a complicated world and make reasonable decisions quickly, and a familiar option often feels like the safer bet. But shortcuts can also introduce bias. When an idea feels unfamiliar, we can mistake our uncertainty about it for evidence that it won't work. That makes it harder to distinguish between an idea that poses a genuine risk and one we simply don't understand well enough yet. And that judgment can happen remarkably quickly.

# When we judge too soon

That speed is where the trouble starts. When a new idea comes up, the questions about what could go wrong often arrive before anyone has asked what is new about it. Someone suggests a different way to handle a process the team has run the same way for years, and within minutes the conversation has turned to risk, cost, and timing. Nobody has rejected the idea outright, but nobody has learned much about it either. In my own work supporting innovation, I see this often among experienced, well-meaning people who sincerely want innovation. The concerns they raise about risk or scope are often fair. What stands out is how early those concerns arrive, usually before anyone has explored what the idea could make possible.

The answer isn't necessarily to say yes to more ideas. As the numbers above show, plenty of new ideas shouldn't move forward. The answer is to make sure we judge an idea after we've learned enough about it, so we're weighing the idea itself and not just its unfamiliarity. Early on, the most honest answer often isn't yes or no. It's that we don't know enough yet.

# Giving new ideas a fair look

When a new idea lands and your first instinct is to push back, a few practices can help slow down that initial judgment long enough to give the idea a fair look.

**Actively look for what's original.** Start by acknowledging the potential for bias and looking for originality on purpose. When a new idea is on the table, say out loud that you want to look at what is original about it, and then do it. Identify what is different, what it could make possible, and why that might matter before turning to concerns. [Licuanan, Dailey and Mumford (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2162-6057.2007.tb01279.x) found that people consistently underestimated the originality of highly original ideas, a pattern they called a "so what" error. That error shrank when people were asked to actively analyze an idea's originality and, in some conditions, when their task was framed around creativity. Being intentional about looking for what is new helped people see it.

**Turn concerns into questions.** If something about an idea makes you uneasy, try to identify what it is and what information would help address it. A vague worry like "this feels risky" is hard to act on, while "we don't know whether users would switch" points to something you can find out. Ask yourself what assumptions you're making about the idea, which of your concerns are supported by evidence, and what you would need to learn to evaluate it more confidently. It can also help to bring in someone from outside the idea's immediate area. As Boudreau and colleagues' research suggests, our proximity to a topic can influence how we evaluate new ideas. The goal isn't to find someone who will evaluate the idea more favorably, but to bring together different perspectives that might uncover possibilities or limitations others have missed.

**Replace assumptions with information. **From there, look for a quick way to learn more. Not every new idea needs a formal business case or a fully developed pilot before we can begin to understand its potential. Many of the questions a new idea raises can be answered in minutes or days, not months. You might ask the person to walk you through the problem the idea solves and who it would help. You could spend fifteen minutes sketching how it might work, find a colleague who has tried something similar, or ask two or three of the people it would affect what they think. Larger steps like a prototype or a pilot can come later if the idea still looks promising. The point is to replace guesses with information, so that whatever you decide is based on the idea itself and not on how unfamiliar it feels.

**Separate exploration from evaluation.** Finally, give the idea room to develop before it's judged. Ideas don't necessarily arrive in their best or most complete form, and evaluating them too early can prevent them from becoming something more promising. Alex Osborn, who popularized brainstorming, built it around deferring judgment so that early criticism wouldn't shut ideas down before they had been explored (Osborn, 1953). Early experiments supported him. [Parnes and Meadow (1959)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0047223) found that people produced more high-quality ideas when they were told to hold off on judging them. [Basadur, Graen and Green (1982)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0030-5073%2882%2990233-1) found that training engineers and scientists in an industrial research organization to separate idea generation from evaluation improved their creative problem solving on the job. In practice, that can be as simple as spending a session building on an idea together before anyone weighs in on it, or scheduling the decision for a later meeting. Deferring judgment doesn't mean eliminating it. It means giving ourselves a better-developed idea to evaluate when the time comes. And if you lead a team, how you respond to the first unusual idea in a meeting tells people whether the next one is worth bringing.

# Curiosity before judgment

We don't need to abandon critical thinking to become more receptive to originality. We need to recognize that our initial reactions to unfamiliar ideas aren't always reliable indicators of their potential. Sometimes the most productive thing we can do is stay curious a little longer.

Karikó's story did eventually turn. In the late 1990s, she struck up a conversation with an immunologist named Drew Weissman while they both waited to copy journal articles, and that conversation became the collaboration behind the mRNA vaccines ([Penn Medicine](https://www.pennmedicine.org/news/internal-newsletters/system-news/2021/november/molecular-discoveries-make-hugs-possible-again)). The copier itself had once been an idea that more than 20 companies turned down ([National Inventors Hall of Fame](https://invent.org/inductees/chester-f-carlson)). Neither breakthrough needed everyone to say yes. Each one needed someone curious enough to take a closer look.
